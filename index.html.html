What is HydroState?

HydroState is a dashboard and statistics tool for analysing shifts in annual streamflow and rainfall data, allowing identification of when catchment runoff generation changed. The underlying method has been widely used to understand when meteorological droughts altered catchment behaviour, and whether catchments recovered from the drought.

HydroState provides:

  • An interactive summary of hydrological state shifts throughout Victoria, Australia. This translates Peterson et al. (2021) in Science and Zahedi (2025) into an interactive dashboard.
  • Application of the hydroState statistical R package through a graphical interface, without the need to install any software or learn R code.

What are hydrological state shifts?

Streamflow depends on rainfall, however, the relationship between the two is not fixed. A catchment can operate in different states, each with its own rainfall–runoff relationship. A state is described relative to a baseline:

  • BaselineThe catchment’s behaviour prior to the Millennium Drought. This is the reference condition catchments are evaluated against.
  • Low or Very LowThe catchment delivers less runoff despite rainfall returning to pre-drought levels.
  • HighThe catchment delivers more runoff than usual for the rainfall received.

A state shift is a lasting move from one state to another, not a single dry or wet year. Non-recovery is when a catchment remains in a lower state after rainfall has returned to pre-drought levels.

How did Victoria’s catchments respond to, and recover from, drought?

Peer-reviewed research (Peterson et al., 2021; Zahedi, 2025) found that the rainfall–runoff relationship in Victoria’s gauged, unregulated catchments was severely affected by the Millennium Drought, and that many catchments appear not to have recovered from the drought that ended in late 2009. Specifically:

  • 57% of catchments shifted to a low-runoff state, many of them very early in the drought.
  • 37% of catchments have not returned to their pre-drought rainfall–runoff behaviour seven years after the drought ended.
  • 80% of those showed no sign of imminent recovery.

The most likely cause is increased evapotranspiration per unit rainfall: vegetation kept using about as much water as before the drought, so more of the rain that falls is taken up by plants and less reaches the stream. Land cover change and groundwater change did not explain the pattern. The three catchments below show what these outcomes look like in practice.

Background & Theory

Streamflow depends on rainfall, but the relationship between the two is not fixed. After prolonged drought, some catchments deliver less runoff for the same rainfall and remain that way for years (Saft et al., 2015). This page builds up the ideas behind that behaviour: catchment states, hydrological resilience, and how Hidden Markov Models detect a shift.

1. States: a Baseline and a departure from it

A catchment can operate in a finite number of distinct states. Each state is defined by how much runoff the catchment produces from a given amount of rainfall, compared with a baseline.

The simplest case has two states:

  • BaselineRainfall converts to runoff as it did before the Millennium Drought.
  • LowThe same rainfall produces less runoff than it used to.

More complex catchments may need a third state:

  • Very LowSubstantially less runoff than the Baseline for the same rainfall.
  • HighMore runoff than the Baseline for the same rainfall.

2. Rainfall changes versus catchment behaviour changes

This distinction is the core idea behind HydroState.

  • Rainfall changes: a dry year produces less runoff because less rainfall occurred. The catchment is still in its Baseline state; it is simply receiving less water. This is expected and is already handled in conventional yield modelling.
  • Catchment behaviour changes: the catchment produces less runoff for the same rainfall. The relationship between rainfall and runoff has shifted. This is a state shift. Across south-eastern Australia, Saft et al. (2015) found this kind of shift in ~46% of the catchment dry periods they studied, with drier, flatter and less forested catchments being the most susceptible.

Plotting each year’s rainfall against streamflow makes the difference visible. Years that follow the usual trend line reflect rainfall alone. Years that sit consistently below it reflect a change in catchment behaviour.

3. Hydrological resilience and tipping points

Catchments are not infinitely resilient. Borrowing from ecology (Scheffer et al., 2001), a catchment can have more than one stable configuration. A large enough disturbance, such as a multi-year drought, can push it past a tipping point into an alternative state that is self-sustaining.

Recovery is not simply the reverse of the shift. Returning to the Baseline state can require wetter conditions than those that triggered the original shift. This asymmetry, called hysteresis, means a catchment can remain in a Low state even after rainfall returns to average (Peterson et al., 2014). These switches can be subtle and hard to spot by eye, which is why a statistical method is needed to detect them.

4. Recovery and non-recovery

During the Millennium Drought (roughly 1997–2009), most Victorian catchments dropped into a lower state. Once the drought broke, they were expected to return to their Baseline state. Some did; many did not.

  • Recovery: the catchment returns to its Baseline state once wetter conditions resume.
  • Non-recovery: the catchment stays in a lower state after rainfall has returned.

Why do some catchments stay low? For Victorian catchments, the most likely explanation is the vegetation (Peterson et al., 2021). Through the drought, plants kept using about as much water as before, even though less rain was falling. More of each year’s rain was taken up by plants and returned to the air, so less reached the streams. This also dried out the ground, so after the drought even more rain was soaked up before it could reach a stream, helping hold the catchment in its low state. Land cover change did not explain the pattern.

5. How a Hidden Markov Model finds states

A catchment’s state cannot be observed directly; only rainfall and streamflow can. A Hidden Markov Model (HMM) is a statistical model built for records that switch between hidden conditions. hydroState uses one to work backwards from the record and estimate which state best explains each year.

Three HMM concepts matter most for interpretation:

  • Hidden states: each state has its own rainfall–runoff relationship. The model estimates these relationships and the state the catchment was most likely in each year.
  • Persistence: states are expected to last. One unusual year is not read as a shift; the model looks for a sustained change in behaviour.
  • Transition probabilities: the model estimates how likely a catchment is to stay in its current state or move to another. A high probability of remaining in a Low state means recovery is likely to be slow.

The result is a timeline for each catchment showing when it was in each state, and when (or if) it shifted. The analysis steps and the maths are in Method & Calculations.

6. What this means for planning

Planning that assumes rainfall will deliver pre-drought runoff may overestimate the water available. Where a catchment sits in a persistent lower-flow state:

  • supply and yield studies should consider runoff assumptions based on the current state rather than the pre-drought record;
  • the probability of remaining in that state indicates how long reduced runoff may persist; and
  • the result supports long-term adaptation planning and stress-testing of supply options.

This aligns with the Victorian Water and Climate Initiative (VicWaCI), which found that streamflow has remained significantly lower than average in many central and western catchments since the Millennium Drought. VicWaCI findings also inform the Victorian guidelines for assessing the impact of climate change on water availability.

This also matters for modelling. Fowler et al. (2020) found that five commonly used rainfall–runoff models could not reproduce the slow, multi-year drying seen during the Millennium Drought, and argued that such models should not be relied on for runoff projections in a drying climate.

Method & Calculations

HydroState uses the hydroState R package to test whether a catchment’s rainfall–runoff relationship has changed over time. The central question: for the same amount of rain, does the catchment still produce the same amount of streamflow?

Lower streamflow in dry years is expected. The concern is a catchment that keeps producing less runoff after rainfall returns to normal. The method separates these two effects.

1The rainfall-runoff relationship

For each time step (year, season or month), streamflow is estimated from rainfall using a straight-line relationship:

Streamflow = a0 + a1 × Rainfall
  • a0 (intercept) sets the baseline level of runoff.
  • a1 (slope) sets how strongly runoff responds to extra rainfall.

Streamflow is usually transformed before fitting (by default, a Box–Cox transform). Streamflow records are skewed, with many modest years and a few very wet ones; the transform evens this out so the relationship fits fairly across wet and dry years. Results are converted back to flow units for display.

Some models also include auto-correlation terms (AR1–AR3). These reflect water carried over in soils and groundwater: a wet year tends to lift flow in the following year.

The error around the line is modelled with either a truncated Gaussian or a Gamma distribution, chosen per catchment.

＋

Conditional mean, AR(p) formulation

μt = αSt + β Pt + ∑i=1p φi (Qt−i − μt−i)

αSt is the state-specific offset: the term carrying the shift in the rainfall–runoff relationship. β is the precipitation coefficient; φi are auto-regressive coefficients for lags 1 to p.

Observation density (selected per catchment)

Qt | St ~ TruncNormal(μt, σ2St, 0, ∞)orGamma(kSt, θSt)

aij = Pr(St = j | St−1 = i)

2Hydrological states

A catchment can operate in a finite number of distinct states, each with its own rainfall–runoff line. In the default model the intercept shifts between states while the rainfall term is shared, so a Low state produces less runoff than the Baseline state from the same rainfall.

† Shifts in the intercept and slope are also the kinds of rainfall–runoff change most likely to be statistically detectable in annual records (Pamminger et al., 2025).

States cannot be measured directly. They are inferred from the record using a Hidden Markov Model (HMM), a statistical model built for series that switch between hidden conditions. Two features matter most for interpretation:

  • Persistence. States are expected to last. By default a state must hold for at least three time steps before a shift is recognised, so one unusual year is not read as a regime change.
  • Transition probabilities. The model estimates how likely a catchment is to stay in its current state or move to another. A high probability of remaining in a Low state means recovery is slow.

3Fitting and choosing the best model

For each catchment, candidate models are built with one, two or three states and different error distributions and auto-correlation terms. Each is fitted by maximum likelihood (finding the parameter values that make the observed record most likely), using a global optimiser with multiple starting points.

The preferred model has the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). AIC rewards good fit but penalises extra complexity, so a two- or three-state model is selected only when the extra states genuinely explain the data better.

If a one-state model is selected, there is no statistical evidence that the rainfall–runoff relationship has changed.

Show the maths: AIC

＋
AIC = 2m − 2 ln(L̂)

m is the number of free parameters, L̂ the maximised likelihood. Adding a state adds an offset, a variance term and transition parameters, so the penalty grows faster than linearly in the number of states.

4Naming the states

States are labelled relative to a reference year. Following Peterson et al. (2021), 1990 is used because it falls before the Millennium Drought. The state occupied in 1990 is named Baseline; other states are named High, Low or Very Low relative to it.

The reference year changes only the names, not the fitted model or the results.

5Checking the model is reliable

Each selected model is checked using pseudo-residuals, which measure how surprising each observation was given the fitted model. For a sound model, pseudo-residuals should:

  • be roughly normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk test p-value above 0.05);
  • show no strong trend over time; and
  • show little correlation from one time step to the next.

The reliability of the state classification can also be tested by generating synthetic records from the fitted model and measuring how often the correct state is identified.

Rejection (small p) indicates the selected model’s error structure is not well specified for that catchment.

6Reading the results

The most likely sequence of states through time is calculated with the Viterbi algorithm. This is the state history shown for each gauge on the Catchment Map.

Each time step also has a conditional state probability showing how confident the model is in the classification. Values close to 1 indicate a clear result; values near 0.5 indicate the catchment sits between states and should be read with caution.

Examples of the three typical outcomes (unchanged, recovered, not recovered) are shown on the Overview tab.

Show the maths: Viterbi decoding

＋

With δt(j) the probability of the most likely path ending in state j at time t:

δt(j) = maxi [ δt−1(i) aij ] bj(Qt)
ψt(j) = argmaxi [ δt−1(i) aij ]

bj(·) is the observation density for state j. The optimal path is recovered by back-tracking through ψ from the terminal state argmaxi δT(i). Computed in log space to avoid underflow over long records.

7Which catchments are included

An initial set of 602 candidate Victorian gauges was screened to 161. Gauges were excluded for insufficient data, upstream regulation or licensed extraction, too short a record around the Millennium Drought, or following local review by catchment management authorities. Full criteria are listed in About.

What the method does and does not show

The method identifies whether and when a catchment’s runoff response changed, and how confident that classification is. It does not, on its own, identify why. For Victorian catchments, Peterson et al. (2021) associated non-recovery with increased evapotranspiration; land cover change did not explain it. Local investigation is still recommended before drawing conclusions for a specific catchment.

Analyse your Data

The same method used for the Catchment Map can be applied to any catchment with a suitable record. Upload or paste an annual record of streamflow and rainfall to test whether the catchment’s rainfall–runoff relationship has shifted, when, by how much, and how strong the evidence is. No software installation or R code is required.

Fit the model

Data requirements
  • Format: annual data, one row per year, with three columns: year, flow and precipitation (in any order, with a heading row). Upload as a CSV, or paste the three columns directly from a spreadsheet.
  • Units: flow and precipitation in the same units, ideally mm/year. Flow in ML/year divided by catchment area in km² gives mm/year.
  • Record length: at least 20 years and no more than 200, including years before the drought, during the drought and, ideally, the years after it. Shorter records should be used with caution because separating a state shift from ordinary year-to-year variation is unreliable.
  • Missing values: gaps in the streamflow record are accepted; leave the cell blank. Gaps in the rainfall record are not allowed.

Steps

  1. Load the data (upload a CSV or paste from a spreadsheet) and check the data preview.

  2. Choose the reference year that defines the Baseline state (1990 by default).

  3. Choose the number of states to test: two by default, or three under Advanced settings. A one-state model (no shift) is fitted automatically alongside for comparison. Fitting can take a few minutes.

  4. Check the model on the Fit Status tab, then review the evidence ratio, state history, classification confidence and size of any shift.

Reading the results

The outputs follow the same conventions as the Catchment Map:

  • Evidence ratio: how many times more likely the state-shift model is than the one-state model. A ratio of 1 or less means there is no statistical evidence that the rainfall–runoff relationship has changed. Peterson et al. (2021) reported results for evidence ratios above 1, above 100 and above 1000, with higher ratios giving greater confidence that the shift is real.
  • State history: the most likely state in each year. The open circle marks the reference year and the bar above the graph marks the Millennium Drought (1997–2009). Years with missing streamflow are shown as gaps.
  • Size of the shift: for each year’s rainfall, the modelled annual flow had the catchment stayed in its Baseline state, compared with the modelled annual flow in the identified (Viterbi) state and with the observed flow. Results are given at the 5th, 25th, 50th, 75th and 95th percentiles; the 25th to 75th percentile range is the range typically used in planning.
  • Classification confidence: the probability of the state shown in each year.

See Method & Calculations for how models are fitted, compared and checked.

On generalisability. The method was validated against the 161-gauge dataset shown on the Catchment Map. Results for uploaded records depend on record length, data quality and degree of regulation, and short or heavily regulated records will be less reliable. The model sees flow and rainfall only, not farm dams, licence changes or a relocated gauge, so results should be combined with local knowledge. Some changes in how rainfall becomes runoff, such as changes in year-to-year variability, may be real but too subtle to detect in records of typical length (Pamminger et al., 2025).

About

Project background, team, references and data sources.

Project background

HydroState is a final-year Honours project in Environmental Engineering at Monash University. It translates the findings of Peterson et al. (2021) and Zahedi (2025) into a dashboard and statistics tool for Victorian water industry professionals, built on the hydroState R package. The platform is being co-designed with industry personnel and is intended as a transferable model for bringing academic hydrological research into operational use.

Intended users

  • Water corporation and catchment management authority planning, technical and engineering staff
  • DEECA and other Victorian Government water policy staff
  • Consulting hydrologists and engineers undertaking yield or supply studies
  • Researchers and students extending the underlying science
